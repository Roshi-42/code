An evidence-led journey through where Australia's frogs are mapped, which species are recorded, and when people listen.

Australia: A Nation of Frogs and Amphibians

From tropical rainforests and wetlands to the temperate south, frogs contribute to the sounds and biodiversity of Australia. Yet the places where different species are mapped are not equally distributed.

Follow the evidence from geographical richness and recorded species, through seasonal and daily recording patterns, to the places where citizen scientists have listened most closely. Along the way, one distinction matters: a recording is evidence of an observation—not a count of frogs living there.

The FrogID citizen-science project provides observations submitted across Australia, allowing us to compare recorded frog diversity between states.

Chart insight

Above or below the model?

Green means more recorded distinct species than the land-area model predicts; brown means fewer. The reference value is 1×. This is a model-relative comparison—not the same as species per 1,000 km².

What Do the Observations Reveal?

Queensland has the highest absolute number of distinct recorded species in this comparison (106). A separate species-per-area calculation puts the ACT first at 6.785 distinct species per 1,000 km², followed by Tasmania (0.175) and Victoria (0.149).

Because the ACT is so small, that measure is especially sensitive to its area; it is different from the observed-to-expected ratio plotted above.

These differences may reflect environmental conditions, geographic distributions and variation in recording effort. They should not be interpreted as a direct measure of total frog populations.

The Final Chorus

What Does Australia's Frog Record Tell Us?

Australia's frogs form a geographically varied chorus, but the chorus captured by citizen scientists is also shaped by where, when and how often people listen.

Place Matters

Mapped richness varies across the country, while Queensland has the greatest number of distinct FrogID-recorded species in the state comparison. Area-normalised and model-relative views answer different questions and can change which places stand out.

Species Matter

The Common Eastern Froglet tops the national record count, but each state's mix of recorded species differs. National totals alone cannot explain local distributions.

Timing Matters

Submitted records are not evenly distributed across the year or the day. They increase strongly toward spring and early summer, and the national evening peak occurs between 8 PM and 9 PM.

The essential distinction: FrogID occurrences measure documented observations. They are not a census of individual animals, and changing record totals should not be interpreted on their own as evidence of population growth or decline.

Where Should the Story Go Next?

To investigate changes in frog populations or conservation status, the next step would be to combine standardised surveys, repeated sampling and information on sampling effort with habitat and environmental data.

Citizen-science data remain invaluable for discovering patterns and guiding questions, provided those limits stay visible.

The most important lesson is not just where Australia's frogs are heard, but how carefully we interpret the sounds people record.
