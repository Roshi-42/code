Australia: A Nation of Frogs and Amphibians

From tropical rainforests to coastal wetlands and inland habitats, Australia's frogs can be find in various environments.

But frog diversity is not distributed equally across the country. Where do the greatest numbers of different species occur, and which states have recorded the widest variety of frogs?

01. Where Is Frog Diversity Greatest?

This map shows how the number of mapped frog species varies geographically across Australia. Darker green regions indicate greater species richness.

Species richness represents the number of different species mapped in a geographic region, not the number of individual frogs. Values have not been normalised for area or recording effort.

Source: Australian Frog Atlas 4.0 (2026).

From Geographic Diversity to Recorded Species

The first map shows how frog species richness varies across Australia. However, another way to understand frog diversity is to examine which species have been recorded by people in different states and territories.

The FrogID citizen-science project provides observations submitted across Australia, allowing us to compare recorded frog diversity between states.

02. Which States Have the Highest Recorded Species Density?

This map compares distinct recorded frog species per 1,000 km² of land area in each state and territory. A logarithmic colour scale makes differences visible across the wide range of values.

Species density = (distinct recorded species ÷ land area in km²) × 1,000. These values describe recorded species richness relative to area, not individual frog abundance. Recording effort varies between regions.

Source: Australian Museum, FrogID Dataset 7.0 (2017–2024). Land areas: Geoscience Australia.

What Do the Observations Reveal?

Queensland has the most recorded species in absolute terms (106). After dividing by land area, ACT has the highest density at 6.785 species per 1,000 km², followed by Tasmania (0.175) and Victoria (0.149). ACT's small area strongly affects this ratio.

These differences may reflect environmental conditions, geographic distributions and variation in recording effort. They should not be interpreted as a direct measure of total frog populations.
